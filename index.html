PROJECT 02

Geometry Zoo

Area formulas through conjecture, verification, transfer, and habitat design

Learners develop area formulas for rectangles, triangles, and circles through direct manipulation and evidence collection rather than receiving the formulas first. Each investigation moves through noticing, conjecturing, verification, explanation, and transfer before learners apply the mathematics to an interactive zoo-habitat design challenge.

JAVASCRIPT GEOMETRY FORMULA DEVELOPMENT DESIGN CHALLENGE
Design focus: Formula certification requires multiple forms of evidence: testing cases, explaining why a relationship generalizes, verifying a geometric derivation, and transferring the formula to a new problem. The culminating habitat project adds competing constraints so learners must optimize rather than simply make an enclosure large enough.

PROJECT 03

Taylor Telescope

Taylor series through live approximation and visual exploration

Learners manipulate the degree of a Maclaurin polynomial and watch it approximate ex, sin(x), cos(x), and arctan(x) in real time. A live graph, error display, derivative-coefficient reveals, and short investigation missions make convergence and local approximation visible.

JAVASCRIPT CALCULUS TAYLOR SERIES LIVE VISUALIZATION
Design focus: Rather than presenting Taylor series as a static formula, learners operate an approximation tool and observe how additional derivative information changes the polynomial's fit, accuracy, and behavior across an interval.
LAUNCH TAYLOR TELESCOPE
PROJECT 04

The Fractal Garden

Fractal geometry through specimen study, recursion, nature, and generative design

A botanical field-journal experience where learners investigate self-similarity, recursion, scale, fractional dimension, branching systems, natural fractal approximations, and the Heighway dragon. A true microscope-style zoom lets learners magnify one fixed Sierpiński specimen rather than merely changing recursion depth.

JAVASCRIPT FRACTAL GEOMETRY RECURSION GENERATIVE DESIGN
Design focus: Exploration culminates in a click-to-plant fractal garden. Learners combine branching trees, recursive ferns, Sierpiński beds, Koch borders, and dragon vines, then compare the recursive rules and distinguish exact self-similarity from the approximate fractal structure found in nature.

Beat the Odds

Probability, simulation, and expected value

Learners choose between competing probability machines, collect their own experimental data, scale simulations from 10 to 10,000 trials, and investigate why winning more often does not necessarily lead to the greatest expected payoff.

PROJECT 06

Trig Park

Trigonometric modeling through an interactive theme-park design studio

Learners design a wave pool, construct a piecewise roller coaster from multiple sine and cosine functions, and animate a Ferris wheel whose motion is linked directly to its sinusoidal height model. Parameter checkpoints require learners to connect amplitude, midline, period, phase shift, and domain restrictions to physical features of each ride.

JAVASCRIPT TRIGONOMETRY MATHEMATICAL MODELING ANIMATION
Design focus: Students do not manipulate parameters in isolation. Each transformation must be interpreted in context, and the piecewise coaster requires learners to coordinate multiple models into one continuous design.
ENTER TRIG PARK
PROJECT 07

The Matrix Market

Matrix operations through an interactive two-day sales-booth simulation

Learners open a market booth, organize sales in labeled 3 × 3 matrices, and perform the matrix arithmetic themselves. Addition combines two days of sales, subtraction exposes increases and decreases, scalar multiplication models changes in market traffic, and a competing-vendor analysis turns the calculations into evidence for a business decision.

JAVASCRIPT MATRICES DATA INTERPRETATION CONTEXTUAL MODELING
Design focus: The simulation does not perform the arithmetic for learners. Students enter every matrix result and receive cell-level verification before the analysis is revealed, preserving procedural fluency while making each operation carry a contextual meaning.

DESIGN PROCESS CASE STUDY

From classroom evidence to interactive design.

Trig Park began as a Precalculus amusement-park modeling project. I taught the original activity, observed how learners interpreted its representations, analyzed student work through my National Board process, and used those observations as design input for the interactive version.

01 · Frame

Identify the conceptual goal and choose a context where the mathematics has a visible physical meaning.

02 · Observe

Study hesitation, misconceptions, questions, representations, and the strategies learners use when they are not yet certain.

03 · Design

Translate those observations into purposeful interactions, checkpoints, feedback, and opportunities to revise.

04 · Iterate

Treat the prototype as a hypothesis and decide what learner behavior should be studied in the next design cycle.

CASE STUDY · TRIG PARK

The original task established the mathematical purpose.

Wave pool → roller coaster → Ferris wheel

The classroom project asked learners to use trigonometric functions to model three amusement-park systems. Students connected amplitude, period or frequency, phase shift, and vertical shift to physical features of a wave pool, roller coaster, and Ferris wheel. Desmos allowed them to test equations, but the paper structure still left some conceptual decisions implicit.

ORIGINAL CLASSROOM TASK LEARNER OBSERVATION NATIONAL BOARD ANALYSIS
Instructional goal: Move trigonometric transformations out of abstraction so learners can explain not only how a parameter changes a graph, but what that change means in a physical model.
LEARNER EVIDENCE → DESIGN RESPONSE

Observed reasoning became interaction design.

Wave pool · Context before calculation: Learners could manipulate parameters procedurally while still needing support interpreting the minimum and midline physically. The simulation now requires them to identify the pool bottom and water height before treating the model as complete.
Ferris wheel · A representation unlocked the model: When a learner was deciding between sine and cosine, sketching the boarding position helped them reason that a ride beginning at its minimum height calls for negative cosine. The digital version makes that choice explicit and then synchronizes the equation with an animated wheel.
Roller coaster · Increase synthesis: The paper task introduced phase shifts and domain restrictions, but a static graph limited construction. The redesigned experience lets learners join multiple sine and cosine functions with separate domains into one piecewise coaster and justify how the segments connect.
DESIGN PRINCIPLES FROM PRACTICE

Teaching experience functions as sustained learner research.

My National Board reflection identified a recurring tendency for learners to seek reassurance before trusting their own reasoning. That observation influences how I design feedback: I prefer constrained hints, checkpoints, visual evidence, and opportunities to revise over interfaces that immediately supply an answer.

EXPLORE → DEVELOP → APPLY SCAFFOLDING LEARNER INDEPENDENCE ITERATION
What I would test next: I would study which parameter checkpoints generate the most revisions, whether the Ferris-wheel animation improves transfer between equation and context, and how learners construct and debug piecewise coaster segments. Those observations would drive the next iteration.

LEARNING DESIGN APPROACH

Interaction should reveal the mathematics.

Across my work, I use interaction as a tool for thinking rather than decoration. Each mechanic is designed to expose a relationship, generate a question, or help learners test an idea.

Explore Before Explain

Learners encounter a mathematical relationship through a problem, visual, or interaction before receiving the formal rule.

Make Thinking Visible

Predictions, explanations, choices, and reflections reveal how learners are reasoning rather than measuring only final answers.

Scaffold Toward Independence

Experiences begin with constrained exploration and gradually remove supports as learners build confidence and transferable understanding.

Formalize After Discovery

Mathematical notation and formulas arrive after learners have developed an intuitive need for them through evidence and pattern recognition.

EVIDENCE FROM PRACTICE

Learning design grounded in real learners

My interactive work is informed by classroom evidence. I use student thinking, assessment data, and iterative reflection to identify where understanding breaks down, design targeted experiences, and refine instruction around what learners actually need.

NATIONAL BOARD WORK

Design for exploration, choice, and productive struggle

As part of my National Board work, I designed a trigonometric amusement-park modeling experience in which learners used Desmos to build wave pools, roller coasters, and Ferris wheels through increasingly complex sine and cosine transformations. I also designed a choice-based trigonometric identity escape room where students selected challenge paths, earned support tools, and learned to persist through non-routine problems.

DATA-INFORMED INTERVENTION

Diagnose before designing

Through targeted PULSE pullouts, I use current class data to identify specific conceptual gaps, create small groups around those needs, and design focused interventions rather than reteaching an entire lesson. This cycle keeps support responsive, timely, and centered on the learner's actual misconception.

LEARNING SYSTEMS

Build structures that expand access

I collaborated with my school and RTI International to develop a First-Gen Framework combining academic intervention, college-readiness supports, and more accessible family communication. I also launched a two-week summer transition program for students preparing to enter Math 171, their first college-level mathematics course.

A continuous design cycle

My National Board reflection work reinforced a process I use across both classroom instruction and interactive content design: understand the learner, make an intentional design choice, observe the resulting thinking, and revise based on evidence.

01 • OBSERVE Notice patterns in student work, misconceptions, confidence, and engagement.
02 • DESIGN Build a representation, sequence, challenge, or support around the learning need.
03 • TEST Put the experience in front of learners and make their reasoning visible.
04 • REFINE Use evidence from performance and interaction to improve the next iteration.
